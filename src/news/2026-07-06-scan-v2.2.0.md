---
title: "Atomdrift Scan v2.2.0: it fetches your dependencies now"
date: 2026-07-06
summary: "Scan stops trusting the manifest — v2.2.0 fetches and scans your dependencies by default, screens them through bloom filters first, and inherits a wave of new formats and byte-level evidence from the engine underneath it."
---

Since its inception, [Atomdrift Scan](/scan/) has answered one question well: is
*this file* hostile? v2.2.0 finally asks the follow-up that matters for
supply-chain attacks — *and what did it drag in with it?*

**It fetches your dependencies now.** Point `atomscan` at a project and it no
longer stops at the files in front of it: an interactive scan pulls and scans
the sample's declared dependencies and install-command packages too, by
default. That's the whole ballgame for modern npm and PyPI attacks, where the
manifest is spotless and the malware rides in three levels down. The obvious
worry is speed, so v2.2.0 puts a bloom filter out front — known-good and
known-bad are checked before any real work happens, and known-good samples are
skipped outright. You pick the appetite with `--mode`: `fast` (bloom only),
`balanced` (the default), or `slow` (always a full scan). Fetched analysis is
cached by content hash and dependencies fetch and scan in parallel, so the
second sweep over a shared lockfile is nearly free.

**New name, two new shortcuts.** The CLI is now `atomscan` — the old `ascan`
was colliding with Avast's `scan`, and the old names still work as aliases. Two
subcommands cut out the download-first dance entirely: `atomscan purl
npm/left-pad@1.3.0` scans a package straight from its coordinate, and
`atomscan url` scans a remote artifact in place.

**It reads formats it couldn't a month ago — for free.** Here's the quiet part:
Scan doesn't parse files itself. [cleave](/cleave/) decomposes them and
[filefacts](/filefacts/) reads the bytes, and Scan classifies whatever they
hand up. So when cleave 2.2.0 and filefacts 1.2.0 learned to read WebAssembly,
Android DEX, CAB and APK archives, OCI container images, and Python sdists,
Scan started catching malware in all of them without a line of scanner code
changing. That's the payoff of the engine split: the scanner gets broader every
time the layer beneath it does.

**And its verdicts now point at the evidence.** Every fact the engine produces
carries a byte-range span, so a `hostile` call traces back to the exact region
of the file that earned it — no more taking the model's word for it. filefacts'
new windowed-entropy scan is the sharpest example: it flags a small encrypted
blob tucked inside an otherwise unremarkable file, the payload-in-a-decoy trick
that whole-file entropy sails right past. Thresholds got recalibrated in the
process (hostile at L25, suspicious at L3000), and a confident-but-opaque ML
"hostile" is now held at *suspicious* rather than cleared — opacity is a
yellow flag, not a green one.

**It's quicker under all of it, too.** [stng](/stng/) v1.7.1 hands the pipeline
a shared string cache — strings get extracted once and every consumer reads the
same copy — and kills a quadratic XOR slowdown that used to turn large payloads
into a coffee break. Rules and models auto-refresh when they go stale
(`--no-update` opts out), so the whole thing quietly keeps itself current.
